Abstract
Magnesium hydride (MgH2 ) is a promising hydrogen storage material due to its high
hydrogen capacity. However, pure MgH2 demonstrates high dehydrogenation temperature
and slow reaction kinetics. To overcome these limitations, in this study, delaminated
Ti3 C2 Tx MXene was incorporated into MgH2 using a simple wet mixing process in order to
improve dehydrogenation performance. It was found that increasing the MXene content
from 1 to 6 wt.% decreased the onset dehydrogenation temperature of the MgH2 -Ti3 C2 Tx
composite from 242 to 218 ◦ C, which is significantly lower than that for pure MgH2 (325 ◦ C).
The activation energy for hydrogen desorption decreased from 145 kJ/mol for materials
containing 1 wt.% Ti3 C2 Tx to 67–70 kJ/mol for materials containing 3 or 6 wt.%, which is
lower than the values reported for ball-milled MXene-MgH2 composites. The wet mixing
process preserves the layered structure of MXene and offers a potentially scalable approach
for industrial hydrogen storage applications.
Keywords: MXene; magnesium hydride; dehydrogenation; composites

The development of environmentally friendly energy sources plays a crucial role in
the face of climate change. Hydrogen is considered one of the most promising energy
sources due to its high energy density, its environmental friendliness, and its potential for
integration into various energy generation systems [1,2].
However, for the widespread implementation of hydrogen energy, a number of technical and economic challenges remain unresolved, the primary one being the safe, efficient,
and reversible storage of hydrogen [3,4]. Traditional storage methods in compressed or
liquefied forms have significant drawbacks: high energy consumption, low storage density, and safety risks [5,6]. Therefore, the focus is shifting to solid-state storage materials
capable of absorbing and releasing hydrogen under moderate conditions. Among such
materials, magnesium hydride (MgH2 ) stands out for its high hydrogen storage capacity
(~7.6 wt.%), good reversibility, and relatively low cost [7–9]. The main obstacles to its practical application remain the high onset temperature of more than 300 ◦ C that is required for

hydrogen release and slow reaction kinetics, which necessitates finding ways to improve
these characteristics [10,11].
One of the most proposed approaches to modifying the dehydrogenation properties
of MgH2 is the addition of various catalysts [12,13]. Recently, particular interest has been
generated by the use of two-dimensional MXene materials, specifically Ti3 C2 Tx , which
possess unique physicochemical properties, such as high electrical conductivity, high
surface area, and the ability for interfacial interaction [14,15]. Ti3 C2 Tx has demonstrated
potential as a catalyst for accelerating the hydrogenation and dehydrogenation processes
in metal hydrides [16–18]. The addition of Ti3 C2 Tx to MgH2 can significantly reduce
the dehydrogenation temperature and improve hydrogen storage kinetics. Ti3 C2 Tx ’s
surface-active groups (e.g., -OH, -F, -O) provide sorption sites for hydrogen molecules and
facilitate their dissociation into hydrogen atoms [19,20]. Simultaneously, its high electrical
conductivity accelerates electron transfer within the material [21]. This not only further
promotes hydrogen dissociation and diffusion, but also significantly reduces the reaction
activation energy for hydrogen sorption and desorption in magnesium hydride [22]. These
combined effects enhance the dehydrogenation reaction kinetics, thereby lowering the
dehydrogenation temperature to ≤250 ◦ C [16,23]. Moreover, the incorporation of MXene
also improves the kinetics of the hydrogenation process, leading to an overall enhancement
in hydrogen storage performance [24].
Adding Ti3 C2 Tx to MgH2 can prevent particle agglomeration and surface oxidation,
which are the main causes of decreased hydrogen storage efficiency after multiple hydrogenation/dehydrogenation cycles. The two-dimensional structure of Ti3 C2 Tx forms a
protective layer on the surface of MgH2 particles, inhibiting their growth and agglomeration, as well as enhancing the chemical stability of the material. Experimental data show
that MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composites retain high hydrogen storage capacity after several cycles,
demonstrating minimal hydrogen capacity loss. This indicates the high stability of such
composites under cyclic operating conditions [25,26].
Liu et al. [23] first introduced Ti3 C2 MXene into the MgH2 system and found that
the addition of 5 wt.% Ti3 C2 reduced the onset dehydrogenation temperature to 185 ◦ C,
which is 93 ◦ C lower than that of pure MgH2 (278 ◦ C), and lowered the activation energy to
98.9 kJ/mol, demonstrating the excellent catalytic effect of Ti3 C2 on MgH2 . Wu et al. [27]
further employed multilayer Ti3 C2 as a catalyst for MgH2 , achieving a lower onset dehydrogenation temperature of 142 ◦ C with 6 wt.% loading, while the activation energy
was 99.0 kJ/mol. Subsequent studies revealed that the catalytic activity of Ti3 C2 Tx can be
further enhanced by controlling the exposed active facets. For instance, MgH2 composites
containing 5 wt.% edge-facet-rich Ti3 C2 Tx achieved an onset temperature of 199 ◦ C and an
activation energy of 78.2 kJ/mol [17]. Moreover, bimetallic MXenes such as Ti2 VC2 exhibit
superior catalytic performance due to the synergistic effect between different transition
metals, with MgH2 composites containing 10 wt.% Ti2 VC2 exhibiting an onset temperature
as low as 170 ◦ C and an activation energy of 71 kJ/mol [28].
In most studies, MXene was mixed with MgH2 by ball milling. Although ball milling
is effective in refining particles and surface activation, the high-energy impact and shear
forces involved can cause fracture or chemical reaction of the two-dimensional MXene
sheets, thereby degrading the layered morphology and causing the change in surface functional groups [29]. Such structural degradation reduces the exposed edge sites and surface
terminations, thereby weakening the catalytic effect of MXene on MgH2 dehydrogenation [21]. In addition, ball milling is energy-intensive and sensitive to milling parameters,
which poses challenges for reproducibility and scalability [30].
In contrast, the wet mixing process adopted in this work offers a gentler alternative.
By avoiding high-energy milling, this liquid-phase route can preserve both the layered

morphology and surface chemistry of Ti3 C2 Tx , while also achieving its uniform dispersion
on MgH2 particles. The intimate interfacial contact in solution is expected to facilitate
catalytic dehydrogenation, thereby improving dehydrogenation properties.
Moreover, most previous studies used relatively high MXene loadings (5 wt.% or
more), and the catalytic behavior of Ti3 C2 Tx at lower loadings remains underexplored.
To address this gap, in the present work, MgH2 –delaminated Ti3 C2 Tx composites with
different MXene contents (1, 3, and 6 wt.%) were prepared by a wet mixing method. The
effect of MXene content on the dehydrogenation temperature and activation energy was
investigated, aiming to provide experimental evidence for the development of efficient
catalysts at low loadings.

2. Materials and Methods
The pristine MgH2 powder (average particle size 40–50 µm) was obtained by hydrogenation of magnesium powder (MPF 4, 99.2% purity, produced in Russia).
For the synthesis of the Ti3 C2 Tx material, a powder of the Ti3 AlC2 MAX-phase with
the average particle size of up to 40 µm (Wuhan Golden Kylin Industry & Trade Co., Ltd.,
Wuhan, China) was used.
First, an etching solution was prepared by mixing 11.65 mL of deionized water, 24 mL
of hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), and 4.35 mL of hydrofluoric acid (HF, 45%), corresponding
to volume percentages of 29% H2 O, 60% HCl, and 11% HF. The Ti3 AlC2 (2 g) powder
was placed in the prepared acid solution and kept under stirring at 200 rpm for 24 h to
remove the aluminum atomic layers from the MAX phase. After etching, the suspension
was centrifuged (3500 rpm, 5 min) to separate the solid product from the acidic supernatant. The precipitate was then washed with deionized water, and the centrifugation
was repeated. This cycle was repeated until the pH of the supernatant reached 6–7. Next,
50 mL of 1M LiCl solution was added to the precipitate and stirred for 24 h for lithium
intercalation. Following intercalation, the aqueous Ti3 C2 Tx –LiCl dispersion was washed
by centrifugation at 3500 rpm to remove excess LiCl. Washing was continued until a
dark, non-sedimenting dispersion of delaminated Ti3 C2 Tx was obtained. The resulting
supernatant was then subjected to several washing cycles by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm,
with water progressively replaced by isopropyl alcohol during each cycle until complete
solvent exchange was achieved. Finally, the sediment containing delaminated Ti3 C2 Tx was
redispersed in anhydrous isopropyl alcohol. The resulting Ti3 C2 Tx material was added to
MgH2 . To improve dispersion and contact between the components, a wet mixing method
in an alcoholic medium was used. MgH2 powder was mixed with the synthesized Ti3 C2 Tx
(1, 3, and 6 wt.%) in anhydrous alcohol, then stirred at 200 rpm on a magnetic stirrer. After
mixing, the suspension was dried under vacuum (approximately −0.1 MPa) at 120 ◦ C for
6 h to completely remove the solvent. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the preparation
process of MgH2 –delaminated Ti3 C2 Tx composites via the wet mixing method.

The phase composition of the MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composites was studied using an XRD7000 diffractometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The measurements were performed using
Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Sample scanning was conducted
in Theta-2Theta mode over an angular range of 2θ from 3.5◦ to 90◦ . The scan rate was
10◦ /min with a step size of 0.0143◦ . The signal accumulation time at each point was 21.49 s.
The morphology of the composite samples was studied by scanning electron microscopy
using VEGA 3 SBU (TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic) equipped with an OXFORD X-Max 50
energy-dispersive spectrometer. Prior to analysis, the samples were mounted on conductive
carbon tape. The accelerating voltage was 20 kV. The study of the specific surface area of
the samples was performed using the low-temperature nitrogen adsorption method on a
Sync 420A analyzer (3P Instruments, Odelzhausen, Germany). TEM and STEM analyses
were performed on a JEM-2100F (HR) microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an
acceleration voltage of 200 kV. The STEM image was acquired in dark-field mode at a
magnification of 30,000×. The HRTEM image was acquired at a magnification of 600,000.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a spectrometer
(SPECS GmbH, Berlin, Germany) equipped with a PHOIBOS-100-MCD-5 hemispherical
analyzer using non-monochromatized Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). The binding
energy scale was calibrated using the Au 4f7/2 (84.0 eV) and Cu 2p3/2 (932.7 eV) lines.
Charge correction was performed using the C 1s line of adventitious carbon (284.5 eV) as
an internal reference. Survey spectra and high-resolution spectra of Al 2p, C 1s, O 1s, Ti 2p,
Mg 1s, F 1s, Cl 2p, and Mg 2p were recorded at a pass energy of 20 eV. The relative surface
elemental composition was determined from the integrated intensities of the photoelectron
lines, corrected by the corresponding atomic sensitivity factors. Prior to measurements, the
samples were degassed at 200 ◦ C for 10 h under vacuum to clean the surface. Measurements
were carried out at the boiling point of nitrogen (77 K) in the relative pressure range of P/P0
= 0.005–0.995. Data from the relative pressure region of 0.05–0.30 were used to calculate the
specific surface area using the BET method. To investigate the temperature and kinetics of
hydrogen desorption from the MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composite, the method of thermal desorption
spectroscopy (TDS) was employed using the Gas Reaction Automated Machine (GRAM,
Tomsk Polytechnic University, Tomsk, Russia) developed by Tomsk Polytechnic University
and equipped with an RGA100 mass spectrometer. The measurements were performed
under vacuum (approximately 10−2 –10−3 Pa) from room temperature to 500 ◦ C at a heating
rate of 4, 6, and 8 ◦ C/min. Hydrogen sorption properties were determined by measuring
the pressure–composition isotherms (PCIs) using the GRC apparatus. The sample was
placed inside the chamber and evacuated to a base pressure lower 10−3 Pa. Then, the sample
was heated to 450 ◦ C with a heating rate of 10 ◦ C/min. The PCI was performed over a
hydrogen pressure range of 0–30 atm, during which the absorption and desorption kinetic
curves were recorded. After each desorption, the sample was cooled under vacuum. This
procedure was repeated for five cycles. To determine the absolute hydrogen concentration
in the composites, the RHEN602 (LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA) hydrogen analyzer was used.

Figure 3 shows SEM images and corresponding EDX elemental maps for the MgH2 –
Ti3 C2 Tx composites with varying Ti3 C2 Tx content. All composites exhibit similar morphology. The average particle sizes are 25, 36, and 16 µm for the 1, 3, and 6 wt.% composites,
respectively. The absence of a clear trend in average particle size may be attributed to
the low MXene content (1–6 wt.%), the gentle nature of the wet mixing process, and the
presence of occasional large MgH2 particles in some samples. For example, the 3 wt.%
composite contains a notably large particle (Figure 3b), which increases the average value.
Since MgH2 is the main component and the MXene content is low, the particle size is
determined primarily by the starting MgH2 powder rather than the MXene content.
Further analysis of the EDX elemental maps reveals that at a Ti3 C2 Tx content of 1 wt.%,
the Ti signal is weak and dispersed. When the content increases to 3 wt.%, the Ti distribution
clearly overlaps with the MgH2 regions, indicating that most MgH2 particles are covered
by Ti3 C2 Tx . At 6 wt.%, the Ti signal is significantly enhanced in the regions of fine MgH2
particles, while the coverage on larger particles becomes uneven, and flake- or block-like
agglomerates appear in some areas (Figure 3c,i), suggesting stacking and aggregation of
MXene layers at higher concentrations. These results indicate that the structure of Ti3 C2 Tx
and its functional groups remain stable after composite synthesis, but agglomeration should
be suppressed at higher MXene loadings.
In general, as the Ti3 C2 Tx content increases, the uniformity of the dispersion deteriorates: from a nearly uniform distribution to the formation of localized aggregation zones.
In this study, local agglomeration occurs at 6 wt.%.
In contrast, most studies on MgH2 –MXene composites focus on catalytic properties
rather than dispersion uniformity [26,27], and the effect of MXene content on dispersion
homogeneity is rarely discussed. The wet mixing method used in this work reveals a
dispersion limit of around 6 wt.% for this method. Notably, the self-assembly approach

Figure 4 shows XRD patterns of the MgH2 -based composites with different contents of
Ti3 C2 Tx . The introduction of MXene into the composite does not change the structure of the
magnesium hydride, which exhibits characteristic peaks corresponding to the tetragonal
structure of MgH2 . A characteristic diffraction peak of the (002) basal plane of Ti3 C2 Tx
MXene can be seen in Figure 4b. The intensity of this peak increases with increasing MXene
content from 1 to 3 wt.%, but shows a slight decrease at 6 wt.%. No peak was detected for
the sample containing 1 wt.% Ti3 C2 Tx .
The lower intensity of the (002) diffraction peak at 6 wt.% could be related to the
local agglomeration observed in the SEM images (Figure 3c,i), which might lead to partial
stacking or disordering of the MXene layers at higher concentrations. However, further
characterization would be needed to confirm this interpretation.

The Ti 2p spectrum of Ti3 C2 Tx shows three main components at 455.4, 457.2, and
459.2 eV (Figure 5a). The component at 455.4 eV corresponds to Ti–C with possible contributions from Ti2+ and Ti–O/Ti–OH terminations [32]. The component at 457.2 eV is
assigned to low-valent Ti3+ , consistent with previous reports [32]. The component at
459.2 eV is attributed to titanium oxyfluoride (TiO2−x Fx ), indicating the coexistence of –O
and –F terminations [27,32].
The F 1s spectrum of Ti3 C2 Tx (Figure 5b) shows two components at 684.8 and 686.5 eV,
assigned to C–Ti–Fx (–F terminations) and TiO2−x Fx , respectively [32]. After composite
formation, the relative intensity of the C–Ti–Fx component decreases, while a new compo-

nent at 688.0 eV appears. This new component is attributed to interfacial fluorine species,
possibly involving F in a mixed Ti/Mg environment, or surface organic fluorine.
The Cl 2p spectrum of Ti3 C2 Tx exhibits a doublet at 199.1 eV, assigned to Ti–Cl
terminations [33]. This signal disappears in the composite, suggesting that –Cl terminations
are removed or replaced during composite formation or their content is under the detection
limit of XPS.
The Mg 2p spectrum of the composite shows a single peak at 49.4 eV, consistent with
Mg2+ [34]. The binding energy is close to both MgH2 (~50.1 eV) and MgO (~50.5 eV), so a
definitive assignment between these species cannot be made from Mg 2p alone.
The O 1s spectrum of Ti3 C2 Tx exhibits two components at 529.9 and 531.9 eV
(Figure 5c). The component at 529.9 eV is assigned to Ti–O species, which may originate from –O terminations or from surface TiO2 . Although no TiO2 crystalline phase was
detected by XRD, its presence cannot be completely excluded. The component at 531.9 eV
is assigned to –OH terminations, with a possible contribution from adsorbed water [32].
In the composite, the O 1s spectrum is dominated by a component at 531.3 eV, assigned
to Mg–O [34]. This signal may originate from partial oxidation of MgH2 to MgO/Mg(OH)2
during sample handling, or from interfacial interaction between Mg and the –O terminations of MXene. These two contributions cannot be distinguished from O 1s alone.
Taken together, the disappearance of Ti signals in the composite (observed in both Ti
2p and O 1s spectra), together with the changes in terminal-group signals observed in F 1s
and Cl 2p, indicate that the MXene surface is most likely covered by MgH2 . Since Ti3 C2 Tx is
confirmed in the bulk by XRD and SEM-EDS, the absence of Ti signals in XPS is attributed
to surface coverage rather than insufficient MXene content. The chemical environment of
the terminal groups (–O, –F, –Cl) is modified, suggesting their possible participation in
interfacial interactions.

Compared with previously reported MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composites prepared by ball
milling, Wu et al. [27] achieved an onset temperature of 142 ◦ C (a reduction of 125 ◦ C)
with 6 wt.% ML-Ti3 C2 , while Gao et al. [17] reported an onset temperature of 199 ◦ C (a
reduction of ~101 ◦ C) with 5 wt.% F-Ti3 C2 Tx . In the present study, it was possible to reduce
the temperature by 107 ◦ C for the materials containing 6 wt.% Ti3 C2 Tx , by using a relatively
simple wet mixing method at ambient temperature, without the need for high-energy ball
milling. Although the absolute onset temperature in this work (218 ◦ C) is higher than in
those studies, the reduction is comparable to that of Gao et al. and only slightly lower than
that of Wu et al., while offering a simpler and more scalable preparation route. Among the
investigated ratios, the lowest onset temperature is observed at 6 wt.% Ti3 C2 Tx , confirming
the catalytic effect of Ti3 C2 Tx on the dehydrogenation kinetics of MgH2 .
As the MXene content increases, the main desorption peak shifts toward lower temperatures, indicating that MXene effectively catalyzes hydrogen desorption processes and
improves the kinetics. In all three composites, two hydrogen desorption peaks are observed
on the thermal desorption curves.
The two-peak behavior may have several possible explanations. First, the intrinsic
multi-step decomposition of MgH2 could produce multiple peaks; however, pure MgH2
exhibits only a single desorption peak under identical conditions (Figure 5a), which rules
out this possibility. Second, the γ/β phase transition of MgH2 has been reported to cause
double peaks in ball-milled samples [36]. In the present work, however, no γ-MgH2 phase
was detected by XRD; its characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 28.5◦ and 32.5◦ are absent from
the XRD patterns, and the wet mixing process does not involve the high-energy milling
required to induce such a transition. Therefore, the two-peak behavior is most likely related
to the non-uniform coverage of individual MgH2 particles by MXene nanosheets. The EDX
maps show no large-scale local enrichment or depletion of MXene, indicating that this
heterogeneity is not a macroscopic compositional fluctuation but rather a local difference in
MXene coverage at the particle scale. The first desorption peak is attributed to the catalytic
effect of Ti3 C2 Tx , whereas the second is likely due to the fact that the MgH2 surface is
not fully encapsulated by the additive. Notably, even the temperature of the second peak
(378–395 ◦ C) is lower than that of pure MgH2 (429–460 ◦ C), suggesting that even regions
not directly covered by MXene may still benefit from partial catalytic effects.
The terminal groups (–F and –O) on the Ti3 C2 Tx MXene surface and the formed
TiO2−x Fx phase act synergistically to enhance the dehydrogenation performance of MgH2 .
It was found that the introduction of 5 wt.% TiO2−x Fx can lower the onset desorption
temperature from 416 ◦ C (pristine MgH2 ) to 189 ◦ C and reduce the apparent activation
energy from 268 to 120 kJ mol−1 [37]. In that case, multivalent Ti species (Ti2+/3+ ) and
oxygen vacancies generated in TiO2−x Fx can accelerate electron transfer and facilitate
hydrogen diffusion. The –F and –O terminal groups on Ti3 C2 Tx can weaken the Mg–H
bond, thereby lowering the barrier and promoting hydrogen recombination. Therefore, it is
suggested that the role of the formed TiO2−x Fx and terminal groups collectively contributes
to the improved desorption properties of the obtained MXene/MgH2 composite.
In the present work, the low-temperature peak is attributed to MgH2 regions in
direct contact with Ti3 C2 Tx , where the catalyst facilitates dehydrogenation, and the hightemperature peak corresponds to regions with insufficient catalytic coverage. This interpretation is supported by the increase in the low-temperature peak area with increasing
MXene content (from 1 to 6 wt.%), indicating more complete coverage of the MgH2 surface
by Ti3 C2 Tx . Furthermore, at the same MXene content, the increase in heating rate leads to a
shift of the desorption peak towards higher temperatures. This is related to limitations in
heat transfer and the specific kinetics of thermal processes [31]. Nevertheless, regardless

of the heating rate, increasing the Ti3 C2 Tx content consistently promotes a decrease in
desorption temperature and enhances the catalytic effect.
To evaluate the efficiency of hydrogen desorption from the MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composite, an analysis of thermally stimulated hydrogen desorption was conducted using the
Kissinger method [38]. Figure 7 shows the plots of ln(β/Tp 2 ) versus 1000/Tp for pure
MgH2 and MgH2 composites with different Ti3 C2 Tx content. The calculated dehydrogenation activation energy values for pristine MgH2 are 189 kJ/mol that is consistent with our
previous studies [39] and other works [40].

The activation energy of the first hydrogen desorption maximum was 145, 70,
and 67 kJ/mol for 1, 3 and 6 wt.% Ti3 C2 Tx , respectively. For comparison, Wu et al. [27]
reported an activation energy of 99 kJ/mol for a ball-milled composite containing 6 wt.%
ML-Ti3 C2 , while Gao et al. [17] reported a value of 78.2 kJ/mol for a composite with 5 wt.%
F-Ti3 C2 Tx . In the present work, the activation energies of the 3 and 6 wt.% composites
are 70 and 67 kJ/mol, respectively, both lower than the literature values, even lower than
the 71 kJ/mol reported by Liu et al. in the bimetallic MXene catalyst system with 10
wt.% Ti2 VC2 added [28]. This indicates that the wet-mixed Ti3 C2 Tx is highly effective in
reducing the kinetic barrier, achieving superior catalytic activity even at lower MXene
content and without high-energy ball milling. The calculated activation energies for the
second maximum were 192, 152, and 155 kJ/mol, which are close to the dehydrogenation
activation energy values for pristine MgH2 . This also confirms the insufficient coverage of
magnesium hydride particles at low MXene concentration in the composite.
The activation energy reflects the intrinsic energy barrier of the dehydrogenation
reaction, which is effectively reduced by the catalytic Ti3 C2 Tx . In addition, the onset
temperature is influenced by physical factors such as particle size and defect density. Varin
et al. reported that when the MgH2 particle size was reduced to approximately 500–600 nm
by ball milling, the onset dehydrogenation temperature decreased by 40–60 ◦ C, which was
attributed to simultaneous particle refinement, defect introduction, and the formation of

https://doi.org/10.3390/hydrogen7040165

Hydrogen 2026, 7, 165

12 of 15

the metastable γ-MgH2 phase [41]. In the present work, the MgH2 matrix was not subjected
to ball milling; therefore, the onset temperature primarily reflects the reduction in the
energy barrier due to chemical catalysis. The experimental results show that the addition
of Ti3 C2 Tx significantly reduces the dehydrogenation energy barrier of MgH2 , especially at
a content of 3 wt.%, where the most pronounced reduction is observed. A further increase
in MXene content does not lead to a notable further decrease in the activation energy.
Figure 8 summarizes the dehydrogenation activation energy as a function of the
onset desorption temperature for the composites in this work and other MXene-based
MgH2 composites from the literature [16,17,23,27,28,42,43]. The activation energies of the 3
and 6 wt.% composites (70 and 67 kJ/mol) are lower than the literature values, indicating
that the wet-mixed Ti3 C2 Tx exhibits excellent catalytic activity even at relatively low MXene
loadings. It should be noted that the literature data shown in Figure 8 were obtained under
different experimental conditions, including various preparation methods, catalyst loadings
and heating rates. Therefore, the comparison provides a general overview of the catalytic
effect of MXene rather than a strict quantitative benchmark.

4. Conclusions
In the present work, MgH2 –Ti3 C2 Tx composites containing 1, 3, and 6 wt% MXene
were obtained using the wet mixing method. The wet mixing method offers a simple,
scalable route for preparing MXene-catalyzed MgH2 composites while preserving the
layered structure of MXene. MXene nanosheets were uniformly distributed in the MgH2
matrix at concentrations of 1 and 3 wt.%, whereas at 6 wt.% local agglomeration was
observed, indicating a dispersion limit of approximately 6 wt.% in this case.
The addition of Ti3 C2 Tx significantly improved the dehydrogenation kinetics. The
onset dehydrogenation temperature decreased from 325 ◦ C for pure MgH2 to 242 ◦ C,
231 ◦ C, and 218 ◦ C for the 1, 3, and 6 wt.% composites, respectively. Pure MgH2 exhibits
a single desorption peak (429–460 ◦ C, depending on the heating rate) with an activation
energy of 189 kJ/mol. In contrast, all composites show two distinct desorption peaks:
a low-temperature peak (306–366 ◦ C) attributed to MXene-catalyzed MgH2 , and a hightemperature peak (378–395 ◦ C) corresponding to uncatalyzed regions. The activation
energy of the low-temperature peak decreases significantly with increasing MXene content,
from 145 kJ/mol at 1 wt.% to 70 and 67 kJ/mol at 3 and 6 wt.%, confirming the catalytic
effect of Ti3 C2 Tx .
Based on XPS analysis it is suggested that the synergetic effect of MXene terminal
groups (mostly -O, -F) and the formed TiO2−x Fx is responsible for enhanced dehydrogenation properties of the MXene/MgH2 composites.
Cycling tests over five sorption/desorption cycles showed stable kinetics, with approximately 0.5 wt.% of irreversibly retained hydrogen and a reversible hydrogen capacity
of approximately 6.4 wt.%.
In future work, longer cycling tests will be performed to assess the long-term stability
and capacity retention of the composite, and more in-depth investigations will be made to
reveal the catalytic mechanism.
