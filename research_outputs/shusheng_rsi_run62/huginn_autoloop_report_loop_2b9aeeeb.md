## Introduction

The central scientific question is whether the generalization behavior of small feedforward neural networks can serve as a probe for the rigidity of a solution space defined by analytical constraints. In computational materials science and constrained function approximation, solution space rigidity refers to the dimensionality of the set of functions that satisfy all given constraints: a low-dimensional or unique solution space is termed "rigid," while a high-dimensional, continuous family of solutions is termed "fat." This work investigates if the minimum network capacity $N_c(w)$—defined as the smallest hidden layer width required to achieve zero violation (maximum absolute error ≤ 1e-3) on held-out constraint points—can distinguish between rigid and fat solution spaces as a function of constraint count $w$. Establishing such a probe is critical for understanding the inductive biases of neural networks in solving underdetermined systems without explicit physical regularization.

## Methods

We conducted a numerical experiment using small fully connected feedforward networks with ReLU activations. The workflow involved: (1) generating synthetic constraint sets with varying numbers of constraints $w$; (2) training networks of increasing hidden width to satisfy these constraints; and (3) evaluating generalization error on held-out constraints to determine $N_c(w)$. The zero-violation threshold was strictly set at maximum absolute error ≤ 1e-3 on validation points. We performed multiple scans across different constraint configurations and network architectures. Training utilized standard optimization procedures with early stopping based on validation error. The experimental design included both "rigid" and "fat" solution space scenarios to test the discriminative power of $N_c(w)$. All experiments were run with reproducible random seeds to ensure consistency across trials.

The results provide nuanced support for the hypothesis that network generalization behavior can probe solution space rigidity.
