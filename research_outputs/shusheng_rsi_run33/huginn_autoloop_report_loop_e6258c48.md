## Introduction

This study investigates whether the generalization behavior of small two-layer feedforward networks can serve as a probe for "rigidity" in the solution space of analytical constraints. In computational materials science and inverse design, solution space rigidity—defined as the dimensionality of the function set satisfying all constraints—dictates how easily a target structure can be recovered from limited data. A rigid (low-dimensional) solution space should be learnable with small networks, while a fat (high-dimensional) one should require infinite capacity or fail to generalize even with large widths. We test this hypothesis by contrasting a rigid family (constant functions, $f'(x)=0$) against a fat family (high-degree polynomials) using capacity scans across varying sample sizes $w$.

## Methods

We define two constraint families via the `family(kind, w, seed)` interface, returning training $(X, y)$ and held-out $(X_v, y_v)$ pairs. Both families are normalized to unit standard deviation to ensure the $10^{-3}$ error threshold is meaningful.

- **Rigid Family**: Constant functions $f(x) = c$. The solution space is 1-dimensional (rigid). We use $w \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ samples to determine $c$ via least-squares fitting, with held-out points sampled uniformly from $[0,1]$.
- **Fat Family**: Polynomials of degree 19, $f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{19} a_k (2x-1)^k$, with coefficients sampled from a standard normal distribution. The solution space is high-dimensional (fat). We use $w=10$ samples (underdetermined relative to degrees of freedom) and hold out 100 points.

Network capacity is scanned over hidden widths $h \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$ using a two-layer tanh architecture trained with L-BFGS-B and multiple random restarts. Zero violation is defined as held-out MAE $\le 10^{-3}$. The minimal width $N_c(w)$ achieving this is recorded. Three random seeds are used per condition to ensure reproducibility.
