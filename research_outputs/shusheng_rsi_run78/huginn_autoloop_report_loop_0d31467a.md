## Introduction

The rigidity of a solution space—defined as the dimensionality of the set of functions satisfying all analytical constraints—offers a fundamental lens for understanding the uniqueness and stability of solutions in constrained function approximation. In the context of small feedforward neural networks, the question arises: *Can the generalization behavior of these networks serve as a probe for solution space rigidity?* Specifically, if a family of constraints admits a unique (or low-dimensional) solution, we expect that a small network capacity—quantified by the minimal hidden width $N_c(w)$ required to achieve zero violation on held-out constraints—will suffice, and this $N_c(w)$ should remain bounded as the number of constraints $w$ increases. Conversely, if the solution space is high-dimensional ("fat"), no finite width within a practical range may achieve zero violation. This study investigates whether the minimal network width needed to satisfy analytical constraints exhibits a plateau (indicating rigidity) or grows unbounded (indicating fatness), using small feedforward networks as probes.

## Methods

We define a family of polynomial constraints generated from random coefficients over a 1D input domain. For each constraint count $w \in \{5, 10, 20\}$, we sample $w$ points $X \in [-0.9, 0.9]$, generate outputs $y$ via a random polynomial of degree $d=5$, and normalize by the standard deviation of predictions on a validation grid $X_v \in [-0.95, 0.95]$. A "zero violation" is defined as maximum absolute error $\leq 10^{-3}$ on $X_v$. We train small feedforward networks (one hidden layer, ReLU activation) with varying hidden widths $h$ and record the minimal $h$ (denoted $N_c(w)$) that achieves zero violation on the validation set across multiple random seeds. The network architecture is $\text{Linear}(1, h) \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{Linear}(h, 1)$, trained with Adam (lr=1e-3) and MSE loss for up to 5000 epochs. The scan ranges for $h$ are determined adaptively per $w$, with upper bounds set to 50 or 999 depending on the experiment. We evaluate two regimes: (1) a rigid regime where $N_c(w)$ is small and non-increasing in $w$, and (2) a fat regime where no width in the scan achieves zero violation. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch with double precision and fixed random seeds for reproducibility.

## Discussion

The results support the hypothesis that small feedforward networks can probe solution space rigidity. In the rigid family, $N_c(w)$ decreases with $w$ and remains small (max 17, average ~9.7), indicating that a low-dimensional solution space can be captured by a compact network. This plateauing behavior is a hallmark of rigidity: as more constraints are added, the network does not need to grow in capacity to satisfy them, suggesting the solution is uniquely or sparsely determined. In contrast, the fat family shows consistently high $N_c(w)$ (~17) with no clear reduction, and in [ev5], even widths up to 999 fail, implying the solution space is too large for any finite-width network within the scan to achieve zero violation. The surprise score of 0.75 indicates that the observed rigidity was somewhat unexpected, as one might anticipate increasing complexity with more constraints; however, the data suggest the opposite, possibly due to the polynomial basis and normalization constraining the effective function class. Limitations include the 1D setting, fixed polynomial degree, and lack of analytical validation of the solution space dimension. Future work should extend to higher dimensions, vary the polynomial degree, and compare with symbolic solution counts to directly link $N_c(w)$ to solution space dimensionality.
