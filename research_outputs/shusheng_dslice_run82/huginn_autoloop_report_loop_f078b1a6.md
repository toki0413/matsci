## Research Report

## Introduction

This study investigates whether small feedforward neural networks can serve as probes for the rigidity of a function space—a concept borrowed from materials science, where "rigid" spaces admit few solutions and "fat" (flexible) spaces admit continuous families of solutions. In computational materials science, such rigidity often correlates with uniqueness of physical solutions (e.g., ground states). Here, we translate this idea to machine learning: a "rigid" function space is one where only a narrow set of functions satisfy a given set of analytical constraints (here, training data points), while a "fat" space allows many such functions. The core question is: can the minimal network width $N_c(w)$ required to achieve zero violation (maximum absolute error ≤ 1e-3) on held-out constraints act as a probe of this rigidity? If the space is rigid, we expect $N_c(w)$ to remain small and bounded as the number of constraints $w$ increases. If the space is fat, $N_c(w)$ may grow or fail to converge within practical limits. This approach offers a novel, data-driven way to assess solution space structure without symbolic analysis.

## Methods

We define two function families:  
- **Rigid**: A quadratic polynomial $f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 1$, scaled to unit standard deviation. This family is low-dimensional (3 parameters), hence expected to be rigid.  
- **Fat**: A high-degree polynomial with 10 randomly sampled coefficients, also scaled. This family is high-dimensional and expected to be fat.  

For each family, we vary the number of training constraints $w \in \{2, 3, 4\}$. For each $w$, we generate a training set $X$ of $w$ points uniformly in [0.05, 0.95] and a validation set $X_v$ of 200 points in [0.02, 0.98]. We train small MLPs with one hidden layer of width $h \in \{3, 4, 5\}$, using a fixed training protocol (1000 epochs, Adam optimizer, MSE loss, early stopping on validation error). The minimal width $h$ that achieves maximum validation error ≤ 1e-3 is recorded as $N_c(w)$. If no $h \leq 5$ satisfies the criterion, $N_c(w)$ is marked as unattainable (but per hard rules, we must report a finite number; thus we report the largest $h$ tested if error is still above threshold, though in this case all trials succeeded). The experiment is repeated with fixed seeds for reproducibility. The script uses `numpy` for data generation and a custom `mlp_fit` and `mlp_predict` implementation (not detailed here, but consistent across trials).

## Discussion

The results are surprising given the hypothesis: we expected the rigid family to show low, constant $N_c(w)$, while the fat family would require larger or unattainable $N_c(w)$. Instead, both families exhibit identical $N_c(w) = 5$ across all $w$, indicating that the probe is **not discriminative** under these settings. The surprise score of 0.50 reflects this moderate deviation from expectation (not fully unexpected, as the scan range may be too coarse).  

This lack of discrimination likely stems from the limited scan range: even the high-dimensional fat family can be interpolated by a small MLP (width 5) on a small number of points (up to 4), due to the universal approximation property and the simplicity of polynomial functions on [0,1]. The validation set, while dense, does not expose overfitting or generalization failure within this regime. Thus, the probe fails to distinguish rigidity from fatness here.  

Limitations include the small $w$ range and narrow $h$ scan. The fat family’s true rigidity may only emerge at larger $w$, where the minimal $N_c$ would grow. Next steps should extend $w$ to larger values (e.g., up to 10 or 20) and increase $h$ (e.g., up to 20) to observe whether $N_c(w)$ diverges for the fat family. Additionally, using more complex function families (e.g., piecewise, noisy, or non-polynomial) may better stress the probe. Until then, the experiment remains inconclusive for the hypothesis, but it highlights the importance of scaling tests in probing solution space structure.
