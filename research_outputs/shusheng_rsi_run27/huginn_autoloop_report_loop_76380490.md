## Introduction
The scientific question is whether the generalization behavior of small feed-forward networks can serve as a probe for the rigidity of a solution space—defined as the dimensionality of the set of functions satisfying all analytical constraints. In computational materials science, such rigidity often arises from geometric or physical constraints (e.g., atomic displacement fields near grain boundaries), where low-dimensional solution spaces imply unique or highly constrained outcomes. By measuring the minimum network capacity $N_c(w)$ required to achieve zero violation on held-out constraints as a function of constraint count $w$, we can distinguish between rigid (low-dimensional, unique) and fat (high-dimensional, continuous family) solution spaces. If $N_c(w)$ remains constant or decreases with increasing $w$, the solution space is rigid; if it increases, the space is fat. This approach provides a model-free, data-driven method to probe solution space structure without relying on physical assumptions.
